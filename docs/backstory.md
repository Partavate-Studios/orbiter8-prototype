# Orbiter 8: Backstory

The story of Orbiter 8, as it stands on 2026-11-07. It's Koda's: change it only when he asks.

- **The story** (below) is the current version. It brings together:
  - the website's published backstory (2026-10-31);
  - the older draft in `story.md` (the Orbiter 8's first flight, the lost Orbiter 6, water);
  - the lore Koda added in `docs/design-ideas.md` (corporate governance, Minerva, pirates);
  - the change Koda made on 2026-11-07: **stars charge the jump drive**; the ships no longer slingshot between stars.
- **What changed** lists each change and where it came from.
- **Calls for Koda** lists the gaps this version fills; each is a guess for him to accept or replace.
- **The website version** keeps the published text as it was, for reference: the website still shows it.

**Why 2140:** it's about when the last bitcoin is expected to be mined. The block reward halves
every 210,000 blocks (about four years), and it reaches zero around 2140.

## The story

It is the year 2140, just a little more than a century after humans began off-world colonization,
and civilization has spread to encompass the whole of the solar system. Expansion began with a brutal
space race, and the first colony on Mars had it rough. They lacked the technology, resources, and
experience to adapt to the rapid and destructive changes in gravity and radiation that caused their
bones to crumble and their muscles to weaken. But while the first generation suffered, the second and
third adapted faster than even the most optimistic 21st century elder could have ever predicted.

As soon as we found ways to exploit the natural resources available in space, we expanded our
colonies to Earth's own moon and the moons of Jupiter, then Venus and the larger asteroids. Genetic
engineering let us take control of our evolution and build bodies made for space: we cured the
cancers that radiation brought, breathed thinner air, and kept every drop of water we drank. Humanity
was not just surviving in space; we were thriving. And our numbers were thriving too. Water became
our most precious resource. It wasn't that we lacked ice; we had more asteroids and frozen lakes than
we could survey. But turning ice into water took work, and work took fuel.

**The corporations.** As the first surge of colonization faded into decades of vicious trade wars,
progress slowed, then stalled. This time became known as The Great Regrettion, after the lingering
regret we couldn't quite articulate. The governments didn't survive it. One by one they failed, and
nothing collapsed, because the corporations had long since taken over every service a government
once ran: water, air, power, transit, courts, schools. People felt uneasy without a government, so
the corporations built one into themselves. Today you are a *citizen of a corporation*. You vote in
it, you pay into it, and it protects you, so long as you live by its laws.

Only one old institution outlived the governments: the **United Solar System Federation**. It governs
nothing anymore. It holds Sol's nine worlds and their stations in common trust, so that no single
corporation can own humanity's home. No one may claim them, and no one may lay siege to them. The
Federation would rather you did not ask.

**Minerva.** Older than the corporate takeover is the **Minerva Galactic Network**: the one place
every corporation agreed to share. It is a ledger, a registry, a court and something more, a special
kind of AI that keeps the books for everyone and belongs to no one. Inter-corporate law runs through
Minerva. Every pilot registers with her; every claim, every name, every siege is filed with her,
stamped, and kept on the record forever. Minerva takes her fees and then destroys them: she keeps
nothing, so no one can buy her. She is neutral, as far as anyone can tell.

**The Orbiters.** Human expansion wouldn't burst again until the next great "land rush" in the early
2090s, when a new line of gravity assist ships, known as "Orbiters", opened new trade routes to the
outer worlds. Slinging from planet to planet, the Orbiters pushed the frontier all the way to the
edge of the solar system. But that was as far as we could go.

Yet again, it seemed that another plateau was reached. We worried that a second Regrettion was
imminent and that maybe we were just too fragile to expand past the barrier of our sun, which, after
all, provides all the energy that sustains every form of life we know. Leaving our planet was the
tiniest of steps compared with leaving our sun.

**The jump.** The answer turned out to be the sun itself. A star pours out more energy in a second
than humanity has ever used. The Orbiter 8 carries a **capacitor bank** that drinks that energy in.
It drops into a close orbit, closer than any ship had dared, and charges from the star's light and
wind. Then it spends all of the charge in a single shot: a jump faster than light, to a neighboring
star. The ship arrives with its banks empty. To jump again, it has to charge again, and the only
place to do that is in orbit around a star. That is why every Orbiter 8 travels **star to star**,
never into the empty dark between them. A bigger bank reaches farther stars, but every jump still
ends at a sun.

The Orbiter 8 was built as a next-generation prototype, with the strongest hull, the most powerful
engines and the most advanced onboard AI of its day: the latest Minerva console. Under the sole
command of that console, the Orbiter 8 dove into a charging orbit around our sun, the second ship
ever to survive one. The first, a customized Orbiter 6, charged, jumped, and was never heard from
again. It is still out there, somewhere in deep space. The Orbiter 8, however, not only left our solar
system; it came back. It arrived at a foreign star in under 36 hours, dropped into orbit, recharged
from a sun no human had ever seen up close, and jumped home. It was the first return trip in history.

**The new land rush.** The first line of interstellar Orbiters is now for sale, and pilots are pouring
out of Sol. On arrival at a star, while the banks charge, the ship's long-range scan sweeps the
neighborhood and charts the stars around it, so every new star opens the way to the next. The stars
nearest Sol already have their old Earth names: Alpha Centauri, Barnard's Star, Wolf 359, Lalande 21185,
Sirius, Ross 154. Everything past them is new. Minerva catalogs each new star by number, "Star 1234",
until a pilot names it. The discoverer names it first, and keeps that right until someone owns it.
Whoever holds all of its planets owns it, until a rival holds most of them.

Pilots claim planets and file the deeds with Minerva. They ferry colonists out from Earth, a hundred
to a hold, and build each new world up from a Citadel to a Colony, a Station, a Port, the Yards and
an Armory. They mount guns on their moons, which no siege can take away, and they fight for what they
hold. Pairs of black holes link far corners of the galaxy: fly into one and you come out of its
partner, somewhere in the dark between the spiral arms. Probably. It has always been fine so far.

**The pirates.** Not everyone signs on. Some people refuse to be a citizen of any corporation. They
denounce every corporate alliance and live outside corporate law, out on the frontier where no
corporation's patrols reach. The corporations call them pirates. The pirates call themselves free.
They are the open-source answer to the corporations, the way Bitcoin once answered the banks.
Corporations patent their designs and license them to their citizens; pirates share theirs openly.
Corporations tax; pirates pool. They organize in **bands** that govern themselves by consensus. No
corporation protects them, so they protect each other. Some of them raid, some of them trade, and
all of them answer to no one but Minerva, the one law nobody can leave.

We are no longer confined to our solar system. The whole of the galaxy now awaits our arrival, one
star at a time. Liberta Galactica!

## Calls for Koda

These fill gaps in what Koda has said. Each is a guess, for him to accept or replace:

1. **The Federation in a world without governments.** Corporate governance says all governments failed, but the game has a Federation that owns Sol.
   - This version keeps the old United Solar System Federation (the name from `story.md`), governing nothing, as the trustee of Sol's worlds so no corporation can own humanity's home.
   - Separately: the title screen's pilot card still says "United Federation of Planets". That's Star Trek's name for its federation, and Koda carried it over from Demo 3; "United Solar System Federation" would match the story. I haven't changed it.
2. **The scan runs while the banks charge.** That gives the 30-second scan an in-world reason. Koda's principle is that every game limit should have one.
3. **Engine range is the size of the capacitor bank.** A bigger bank reaches farther stars; the Yards sell range upgrades.
4. **Black holes need no charge.** They're natural bridges, which is why going through is clean (D39) and needs no star. The story doesn't say this outright; it fits if Koda wants it.
5. **Minerva destroys her fees** so that no one can buy her. That's the in-world reason for the burned fees.
6. **Pirates answer to Minerva.** Even outside corporate law, the network's rules (the contracts) still bind them. That's Minerva-as-the-blockchain from `design-ideas.md`.
7. **`story.md` is superseded** by this file. Koda can delete it or keep it as a draft.

**In the game, unchanged:** one jump tip says the 2090s Orbiters were gravity-assist ships. That's still true in this version, because they only flew between planets. Koda said the UI doesn't need to tell the charging story, so no player-facing text changed.

## The website version (2026-10-31)

The backstory as published on the Orbiter 8 website, shared by Koda on 2026-10-31. It's kept here
as it was, with one typo fixed ("Obiter" for "Orbiter"). Two lines between paragraphs on the website,
"Planets Menu" and "space", were image captions and are left out.

It is the year 2140, just a little more than a century after humans began off-world colonization, and civilization has spread to encompass the whole of the solar system. They lacked the technology, resources, and experience to adapt to the rapid and destructive changes in gravity and radiation that caused their bones to crumble and their muscles to weaken. This time became known as The Great Regrettion, tied to the lingering feeling of regret that we couldn’t quite articulate. Human expansion wouldn’t burst again until the next great “land rush” in the early 2090’s when a new line of gravity assist ships, known as "Orbiters", opened new trade routes to the outer worlds. Thanks to the Orbiter ships, the human frontier pushed further outward, all the way to the edge of the solar system. But that was as far as we could go.

Liberta Galactica!

## How it lines up with the title's Story scene

The title's Story scene was built from the Multiverse Portals demo's storyboard (see
`UPGRADE_NOTES.md`, "Up next"); the scene doesn't need to tell all of the story. The storyboard's
first three panels follow this arc. Panels 4–6 are the hackathon pitch ("portals", "an MMO for the
EVM") and don't belong to this story.

| Panel | Says | In the story |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "By the year 2140…" | civilization spans the solar system |
| 2 | "Still bound to our sun…" | the second plateau: the sun as the barrier |
| 3 | "That was until today! The first starships have arrived." | the Orbiter 8's first jump and return |

Beats a short scene could use: Mars's hard first colony, the Great Regrettion and the corporations,
the Orbiters opening the outer worlds in the 2090s, the sun as both the barrier and the way out (the
charging orbit), and the closing "Liberta Galactica!", which the game already uses when a new ship
launches.
